From ideal heightmap to credible forming tool

Design-rule checks, material behaviour, springback compensation, calibration, alignment, and a practical product roadmap for browser-generated paired dies.

Core decision

Do not turn a generic material dropdown into an implied forming simulation. Build a layered system that tells users what is known, what is a useful warning, what was calibrated, and what requires nonlinear FEA.

Why this matters NIST describes springback as the elastic change after unloading and attributes its poor predictability to residual stress and multiaxial, non-linear strain paths. Alloy, temper, thickness, grain direction, friction, edge restraint, load history, and tool compliance all matter. A single “steel compensation” percentage would be misleading.

A useful DRC framework

Separate hard failures from process-dependent advice. Every result should identify its confidence level and display the affected region as a selectable heatmap in the existing 3D preview.

P0Gap and collision residualHard checkDoes the discretized die pair preserve requested material separation everywhere?Closest-point/BVH distance map; report minimum and percentiles. Test die/die and die/sheet intersections.

P1Slope / withdrawalAdvisoryAre near-vertical regions difficult to release, print, or inspect?Map n · pressAxis. True undercuts are errors; a maximum slope/draft threshold is user- and process-defined.
P1Minimum radiusAdvisoryAre bends sharper than the material, printer, or offset can support?Map Rmin = 1 / max(|k₁|, |k₂|) against exact alloy/temper/orientation and manufacturing thresholds.

Errors should prevent export or require explicit override. Warnings are process-specific and should never silently alter the artwork. Information layers describe complexity without claiming failure.

Turn DRC into useful correction tools

A warning-only system will frustrate users. Add a non-destructive “condition heightmap” stack that previews deviation from the original and preserves the existing zero-height and peak-to-peak contract.

  1. Physical low-pass filter: remove spatial frequencies below printer/process tolerance, expressed in millimetres rather than pixels.
  2. Minimum-radius filter: penalize excessive Hessian/principal curvature while retaining broad relief.
  3. Slope limiter: project height gradients to a configurable limit and solve a smooth reconstruction.
  4. Island/groove cleanup: morphological opening/closing at the declared tool radius to remove features that cannot survive manufacturing.
  5. Deviation report: show maximum, RMS, and percentile height errors plus a difference heatmap before applying the result.

A GPU ping-pong optimization can minimize target deviation plus smoothness while projecting slope/curvature constraints. This is a controlled geometric approximation, not forming simulation.

Keep the current spherical-envelope method The research supports the Minkowski/spherical-envelope approach over vertex-normal displacement. Add a DRC warning that a true Euclidean offset may cease to be a heightfield when curvature is high or topology changes.

Material profiles, not magical presets

Offer convenient starting profiles, but require users to select or enter the exact grade, condition, thickness, and direction before enabling predictive warnings. Broad categories should carry a “screening only” badge.

Profile schema

category · grade/alloy · temper · thickness · E · ν · density · yield curve · UTS/elongation · hardening law · r-values 0/45/90 · FLC points · min r/t by grain angle · friction · thinning threshold · process pressure range · source/revision · confidence

Paper profiles need a different schema: basis weight, caliper, MD/CD axes, RH/moisture conditioning, compression response, and an empirical pressure window. Paper is orthotropic, compressible, inelastic, and moisture-sensitive; it should not reuse metal plasticity fields.

Useful preset UX Ship “uncoated paper”, “cotton paper”, “paperboard”, “soft cartridge brass”, “forming aluminium”, and “low-carbon steel” as editable templates. Require confirmation of thickness and condition, show sources and revision dates, and let users duplicate profiles into a local calibration library.

Springback: three credible levels

1. Local screening estimate

Show the qualitative risk of elastic recovery using local radius, thickness, elastic modulus, and yield strength. Springback generally increases with higher yield strength and larger radius-to-thickness ratio, and decreases with thickness. Use this to rank regions, not to generate an “accurate” compensated die.

2. Empirical calibration

This is the best near-term product feature. Generate a compact coupon containing the same range of radii, slopes, feature sizes, and depths as the user’s artwork. The user forms it with the actual material, press, lubricant, hold time, and printed-tool process, then enters measured heights/radii or imports a scan.

  • Fit a bounded depth/radius correction curve and report residual error.
  • Key the profile by exact material, thickness, grain direction, tool print process, press, lubrication, closure, and hold settings.
  • Allow multiple calibration runs and display confidence intervals rather than hiding variance.
  • Apply correction non-destructively and rerun DRC after every iteration.

3. External forming simulation

For serious metal work, export the blank, rigid die surfaces, press stroke, contact/friction, material axes, and material law to a nonlinear forming solver. The credible workflow is forming/contact followed by unloaded equilibrium, then iterative tool morphing:

tool(next) = tool(current) + gain × regularize(target − simulated_unloaded_shape)

Repeat forming and unloading until the deviation is within tolerance. Commercial forming tools such as LS-DYNA, Ansys Forming, Abaqus, PAM-STAMP, and DYNAFORM are built for this class of problem. Relief Press should initially export neutral geometry plus a versioned JSON manifest, then import the simulated final sheet or deviation field.

Bottoming and coining These processes may reduce springback but substantially increase pressure, marking, thinning, and printed-tool risk. Model them as explicit process choices with warnings, never as an automatic compensation toggle.

Alignment, registration, and die-set features

The most immediately useful tooling addition is not a springback model. It is repeatable registration. Official locating guidance supports a two-locator scheme: one round pin constrains two axes and one diamond/slotted receiver constrains the remaining axis without over-constraining hole spacing.

Precision stripper guides use micrometre-scale clearances, far beyond typical printed pin accuracy. Hardware presets should therefore generate standard holes and receiver geometry, with printable alignment labelled as prototype-grade.

Press force and printed-tool risk

Do not issue a confident tonnage number from heightmap area alone. Contact develops progressively; metal flow stress, friction, edge restraint, geometry, coining, and printed-tool compliance dominate. A simple F = nominal pressure × projected area value can be shown only as an editable empirical estimate with a safety factor.

Near-term checks

  • Ask for press rated capacity, daylight, bed size, and maximum stroke.
  • Ask for tool process: FDM, SLA/resin, metal print, machined polymer, or machined metal.
  • Check backing thickness, unsupported spans, sharp re-entrant corners, print orientation, and local remaining wall.
  • Show a risk index from formed area, maximum slope, minimum radius, depth/thickness, and material ductility class rather than fake precision.
  • Let users record measured press force during coupon trials and fit an empirical estimate for that setup.

Published rapid-tooling work demonstrates that printed polymer tools can form aluminium in short-run experiments, but strength, proportional limit, wear, print orientation, and process variability constrain their use. Printed tools should be described as prototype/short-run tooling unless the user supplies validated tool data.

Safety boundary The software cannot certify a press setup. Export should include a checklist covering rated tooling/press capacity, secure mounting, guards or safeguarding devices, thin/soft trial blanks, compatible lubrication, incremental strokes, crack inspection, and lockout/safety blocks for setup. OSHA requires point-of-operation safeguarding for mechanical power presses.

Suggested next engineering milestone

Implement Phase 1 and the heatmap shell of Phase 2 together. Add a DRC drawer listing issues, a heatmap selector in the preview, per-check confidence labels, and an export gate for hard errors. This creates the architecture that every later material, calibration, and tooling feature can reuse.
